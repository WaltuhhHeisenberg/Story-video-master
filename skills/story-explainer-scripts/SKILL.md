# Story explainer scripts

This skill writes short(default: 5 minutes) narrated scripts about real/imagined/or adapted events: "the contest that killed five people", "the launch that went wrong", "the recall that cost billions", a story adapted from "The Gift Of Magi". It encodes how this style holds attention, sentence by sentence, so that a viewer never hits a spot where they can reasonably think "so what?" or "I get it, move on." It often involves death.

The structure comes from a sentence-level analysis of two full transcripts (a balloon-launch disaster and a bottle-cap contest riot), the short-form cutdown of the first, and the opening of a third video. It captures mechanics, not wording. Never reproduce or closely mirror any creator's sentences; write original sentences that use the same moves. The evidence base is small, so treat the patterns as strong defaults, not laws, and bend them when a story demands it.

## The model in five lines

1. **State the outcome first.** The title and first sentence give away what happened (usually that it ended badly). The viewer then stays for how and why, which is a stronger pull than what.
2. **Every sentence pays off one thing and opens one thing.** A sentence that does neither is dead weight and gets cut or rewritten.
3. **Expectation, then violation.** State what was supposed to happen, plainly, then show what happened instead. Most of the drama is the gap.
4. **Numbers carry the argument, not adjectives.** Contrasts such as cost versus penalty or planned versus actual make the point without the narrator editorializing.
5. **Plain voice, strong order.** Emotion comes from the sequence of events and from other people's voices, so the narrator can stay flat and factual.
6. End on a short punchy, ironic line.

Why this works: attention spikes when a prediction fails and sags when information merely accumulates. The structure below keeps manufacturing small failed predictions while the viewer already knows where the story is heading.

## Workflow

Follow these steps in order. Do not start drafting before the fact ledger exists, because every number in this style is a load-bearing sentence.

### 1. Intake
Find out: the topic, the format (long-form about 750 to 850 words, or Short about 80 to 150 words), whether there are clips or screenshots available to use as archive moments, and any tone or sensitivity limits. If the topic is missing, ask one short question. Otherwise state your assumptions in a line and proceed.

### 2. Research and build the fact ledger
Search for the event if tools are available. Build a table: claim, source, confidence (confirmed, reported, alleged, disputed). Use primary or high-quality sources where you can. Triangulate numbers across at least two sources, since figures often differ between reports. See "Honesty and care" below. Do not write from memory for dates, counts, money, or names.

### 3. Find the story engine
Answer these before outlining. They decide whether the story works:
- **Expectation pair:** What did people expect would happen, and what happened instead? This becomes the hinge.
- **Motive:** Why did the key people want this? A reputation, a rivalry, a budget, a fear. Motive gives the stakes a person.
- **Reasonable decisions:** Which choices made sense at the time? List them. Disasters feel earned, not random, when each step was understandable.
- **Second thread:** Is there a parallel story that quietly collides with the main one? If yes, it is usually the strongest structural choice available.
- **Contrast number:** Is there a pair of figures whose gap makes the point (promised versus paid, planned versus actual)?
- **Coda:** What is the final consequence that makes the event bigger than itself (a rule changed, a category ended, an apology that says nothing)?
- **The unproven twist:** Is there an allegation you cannot confirm? If so, include it only as an allegation, then let it deflate honestly rather than oversell it.
- **Adapted Story**

### 4. Build the beat map
Use the template below, scaled to the story. Not every story has every beat, and some beats expand (a long legal aftermath, a two-thread middle). Write the beat map first, show it briefly, then draft.

### 5. Draft sentence by sentence
Use the sentence shapes and voice rules below. Read `references/sentence-shapes.md` for templates and `references/hooks.md` for the opening. Draft the hook last-but-first: write three options, pick one.

### 6. Audit
Run the audit in `references/audit.md` before delivering. Fix dead sentences, unpaid hooks, and unverified numbers. This step is where most of the quality comes from.

### 7. Deliver
See "Output format".

## Beat template (long-form, about 750 to 850 words)

At roughly 150 spoken words per minute, 5 minutes is about 750 words. The analyzed scripts averaged 14 to 16 words per sentence, with sentences ranging from two or three words up to about thirty.

| Beat | Sentences | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Cold open | 1 to 2 | Year or date, familiar thing, scale, outcome. Flat and specific. |
| 2. Name and rule or motive | 3 to 6 | Name the thing, explain how it works or why they wanted it, in plain words. |
| 3. Ambition and build-up | 4 to 8 | Escalate with hard numbers and one concrete detail per escalation. |
| 4. Second thread (if any) | 4 to 8 | Introduce the parallel story with a clear cut ("meanwhile"). Plant details that will matter later. |
| 5. Fake relief, forced choice | 3 to 5 | Things seem fine, then a constraint removes the safe option. |
| 6. Tiny triumph | 1 to 3 | A brief peak of joy or success. Keep it short so the drop lands. |
| 7. Hinge | 3 to 4 | Signpost the turn, state the expectation, break it. |
| 8. Cascade | 5 to 10 | Consequences escalate in scale, each concrete and visual. |
| 9. Collision and cost | 3 to 6 | Threads meet; state the human cost plainly in one sentence. |
| 10. Aftermath arc | 4 to 15 | Time jumps, legal or institutional response, the contrast number, one honest deflation if there is an unproven claim. |
| 11. Coda | 2 to 4 | A widening consequence or an institution's own words. No moral. |

Put the contrast number late (it works as a second payoff after the main event). Put the strongest detail of each beat at the end of its last sentence.

## Hooks: the first one to two sentences

Aim for 15 to 30 words. Include a date or year, a familiar thing or company, a number, and the serious outcome. No greeting, no channel intro, no "today we're going to talk about". Pick one of three formulas:

- **Flat shock:** ordinary thing + year + number + deadly or costly outcome, in one sentence.
- **Happy frame, then flip:** describe the hopeful event warmly in sentence one, then say that instead of the hoped-for result it became the disaster.
- **Answer, then doubt:** name the obvious culprit, then ask whether it really was. This is the one formula that uses explicit questions.

The next sentences must name the thing, explain the rule or motive, and begin the story. The hook is the start of the content, not a pause before it. Details and rewrites are in `references/hooks.md`.

## Sentence shapes

Use these deliberately; most sentences in a good script are one of them.

1. **Fact, then reversal clause.** Expected frame first, flip in the last words.
2. **Bare hook.** Announces a turn without its content ("this is where it went wrong"). Owes a payoff within about ten sentences. Use two or three per script, not more.
3. **Delayed explainer.** After a bare hook, spend one to three sentences on the mechanism before showing the damage, so the break feels logical.
4. **Number-last punch.** Put the biggest figure at the end of the sentence.
5. **Long, then short.** A mechanism sentence followed by a two- to four-word verdict.
6. **Voice swap.** A clip, reporter, or official statement carries the emotion while the narrator stays flat.
7. **Plain statement.** The most painful fact, in one unadorned sentence, after the build-up has done the work.

Run loops at three lengths at once: micro (a fact plus a flip), mid (a bare hook paid off over five to ten sentences), and long (a thread opened early and paid off near the end). Open the next loop before the previous one closes, so there is never a natural place to stop watching.

## Voice rules

- One fact or one turn per sentence. Short sentences deliver verdicts; longer ones explain mechanisms.
- Chronological order, but connected by causal hinges ("but", "so", "instead", "meanwhile", "ironically"), never a chain of "and then".
- Announce hinges. Telling the viewer a turn is coming raises attention and costs nothing.
- Use numbers and dates instead of adjectives. Roughly one sentence in four carries a hard figure or date.
- One vivid, slightly odd detail per escalation (a bulldozer clearing balloons, people digging through dumpsters). Specific details make abstract scale real.
- State the opposing side's logic in a sentence, fairly. Perspective-taking makes the narrator trustworthy.
- At most one editorial wink in the whole script.
- Keep the triumph to one to three sentences.
- Slow down for pivots (exact time, a meeting, a single moment) and compress everything else ("over the next year", "in 2006").
- No moral, no lesson, no summary, and no call to action inside the story. If the user wants a call to action, add it as a separate plain line after the final sentence.
- End the script with an ironic, punchy and hard hitting line.

## Honesty and care (real events, often involving deaths)

This style trades on stakes involving real people, so accuracy is part of the craft.

- Never invent dialogue, scenes, motives, or numbers. Everything in the script traces to the fact ledger.
- Label allegations as allegations ("a police officer alleged that...") and disputed numbers as reported. An honest deflation of an unproven claim makes the confirmed claims more credible.
- If you reconstruct a scene for visuals, flag it as a dramatization in a production note rather than presenting it as fact.
- Name victims only when sources do, and state deaths plainly without gore or mockery. Do not blame named individuals beyond what sources establish.
- Original expression only: use the structure, not any existing video's phrasing, and never copy a transcript.

## Short-form version (30 to 60 seconds)

Skeleton, about 80 to 150 words: **striking image or fact** (with number) → **"seems harmless" framing** → **the expectation** → **but/instead, the violation** → **one-sentence consequence** → **pointer to the full story** (only if a long version exists). One loop only; no second thread.

## Output format

Deliver, in this order:
1. **Promise and engine:** the title promise and one line naming the expectation pair.
2. **Fact ledger:** compact table (claim, source, confidence). Flag anything unconfirmed.
3. **Beat map:** the beats used, with approximate sentence counts.
4. **The script:** clean narration, with bracketed cues only where needed ([ARCHIVE CLIP: description], [ON-SCREEN TEXT: ...], [DRAMATIZED]). Show a word count and an estimated runtime at about 150 wpm.
5. **Notes:** where each bare hook pays off, and anything you changed during the audit. Include a sentence-by-sentence table only if the user asks for it or if they gave you a script to audit.
6. **Optional:** three alternative hooks and a Short cutdown, when the user asks or when it clearly helps.

## Common failure modes

- **A warm-up intro.** The first sentence must already be the story.
- **Hiding the outcome.** Spoil the what, never the how. The mechanism is what the viewer waits for.
- **A long triumph.** It dilutes the fall.
- **Adjective-driven emotion.** Replace "horrifying" with the fact that was horrifying.
- **Every sentence the same length.** Vary the rhythm; follow long mechanism sentences with a short verdict.
- **Bare hooks that never pay off,** or paying off a hook and opening nothing next.
- **Too many threads.** One main thread plus at most one parallel thread in a five-minute script.
- **A summary ending or a moral.** End on a consequence or an institution's own statement.
