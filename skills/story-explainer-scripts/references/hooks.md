## 1. What the analyzed openings did

- **Flat shock (Pepsi contest video):** one sentence of about 17 words gave the year, the brand, the country, and the fact that the contest ended up killing five people. No greeting, no setup.
- **Happy frame, then flip (balloon-disaster video):** sentence one described a hopeful charity launch with a scale number; sentence two said that instead of an impressive spectacle it would be remembered as a fatal disaster. The hook is the gap between the two.
- **Answer, then doubt (Toyota video, from the opening of its description):** a date, a family killed, the apparent culprit teased as a stuck pedal, then the fallout in numbers, then two direct questions about what actually went wrong and whether the company was to blame.
- **Short-form cutdown:** shows the launch, calls it a harmless-looking event, states what was expected, says weather forced the balloons down, then points to the full story. The same hook compressed into one breath.

The common thread: the viewer learns within seconds that something went badly wrong, so the open question is how, which is a stronger reason to stay than what.

## 2. Ingredients, in order of importance

1. **The outcome comes first.** The title already promises it; the first sentence confirms it. This removes the main reason to leave early (not knowing whether it is worth it).
2. **An ordinary thing paired with an extreme result.** A soda contest, a charity launch, a family car. The viewer holds a mild, familiar picture, and the line breaks it. The gap is the hook.
3. **Hard numbers and dates in the first lines.** A year, a count, a sum. Specifics make it feel documented and not rumored.
4. **No throat-clearing.** No greeting, no "today we're going to talk about", no "you won't believe". The first words are the story.
5. **The title's promise is confirmed at once.** If the title says "the contest that killed five people", the first sentence says it in plain words, so the viewer knows they are in the right place.
6. **Keep delivering.** The next two to five sentences name the thing, explain the rule or motive, and start the story. Do not circle back to explain what is coming.

## 3. Three formulas with templates

**Flat shock**
> In [year], [familiar company or place] [did ordinary thing] that ended up [serious outcome with number].

Use when the outcome is startling on its own and the title makes the same promise.

**Happy frame, then flip**
> In [year], [hopeful event with scale number], [stated hopeful purpose]. But instead of [the hoped-for result], it would go down as [the disaster].

Use when the event looked like a success or a good idea. The first sentence should sound genuinely positive so the flip has something to break.

**Answer, then doubt**
> On [date], [people] were [harmed] in [event]. The culprit? [Obvious cause]. [One sentence of fallout with numbers.] But what actually went wrong? And was [the accused] really to blame?

Use when the cause is contested. This is the one formula that benefits from explicit questions, because the story is an investigation. Give the obvious answer first so the viewer is briefly satisfied, then reopen the gap.

## 4. Worked examples

These are illustrative. Verify every fact against sources before using any of them in a real script.

*Flat shock:* "In 1919, a storage tank in Boston burst open and sent a wave of molasses through the streets, killing 21 people."

*Happy frame, then flip:* "In [year], a city launched [number] [objects] as a feel-good fundraiser. But instead of an impressive spectacle, it would be remembered for [outcome]."

*Answer, then doubt:* "On [date], [family] died when [cause the public blamed]. The company paid [number]. But what really happened that day? And was it really to blame?"

## 5. Bad-to-better rewrites

| Weak opening | Why it fails | Better |
|---|---|---|
| "Hey guys, today we're covering a crazy story about a soda company." | Greeting, no stakes, no number | "In 1992, a soda company ran a contest that ended up killing five people." |
| "This story will shock you, so stay till the end." | Promises emotion instead of delivering a fact | Replace with the fact that is shocking. |
| "A terrible disaster happened in a big city many years ago." | Vague; no year, place, or scale | "In 1986, 1.5 million balloons were released over a single city." |
| "Have you ever wondered how a contest could go wrong?" | A weak generic question | State what went wrong, then let the viewer ask how. |
| Starts with the company's founding in 1950 | Backstory before stakes | Start at the incident; fold history into the motive beat. |

## 6. The hook test

Read only the first two sentences and check:
- Could someone tell what happened and roughly when and where?
- Is there at least one hard number or date?
- Is the outcome stated, not hinted?
- Is there a gap (ordinary frame versus extreme result, or answer versus doubt)?
- Is there nothing in it that could be deleted without losing a fact?
- Does it match the title's promise?

If any answer is no, rewrite.

## 7. Writing three options

Always write three hooks, one per formula where the story permits, then choose. Choose the one whose gap is widest and whose first sentence a stranger could understand with no context. If the story has a contested cause, prefer "answer, then doubt." If the event looked like a success, prefer "happy frame, then flip." Otherwise default to flat shock.
