## 1. The core mechanism: pay one thing, open one thing

In the analyzed scripts almost no sentence only informs. Each one answers the question the previous sentence raised and plants the next question. When drafting, label every sentence with two short notes:

- **Pays:** what question from earlier does this answer?
- **Opens:** what new question does this raise?

A sentence with neither is dead weight. A sentence that only opens (a bare hook) is allowed occasionally, but it creates a debt that must be paid within about ten sentences. A sentence that only pays and opens nothing is a place the viewer can stop watching.

The opening of a typical script chains like this: the first sentence pays "what happened?" and opens "how could that happen?"; the next sentences pay "what is it?" and open "why would people care?"; the motive sentence pays that and opens "how far will they go?"; a scale statistic pays that and opens "what does the company do now?". Every link hands a question to the next.

## 2. The seven shapes, with templates

**1. Fact, then reversal clause**
Template: "[Expected frame], but [reversal in the last words]." or "Instead of [hoped result], it [actual result]."
Rule: the surprising word sits last, so the sentence pays off its setup and hooks the viewer in one breath.

**2. Bare hook**
Template: "This is where things went wrong." / "Then a new twist surfaced."
Rule: announces a turn without its content. Use two or three per script. Each owes a payoff within about ten sentences. Never stack two in a row.

**3. Delayed explainer**
Template: after a bare hook, "[Who] controlled [the system]. [How the system worked]. [Why that kept it safe or within budget]."
Rule: one to three sentences on the mechanism before the damage. The viewer now understands the rules, so the failure feels logical and earned.

**4. Number-last punch**
Template: "[Context clause], which meant [count] [objects] were in circulation."
Rule: put the biggest figure at the end of the sentence, where attention peaks. Follow with a human-scale detail ("many people held more than one") to make the number feel real.

**5. Long, then short**
Template: "[Longer sentence explaining the mechanism and its effects.] [Two- to four-word verdict.]"
Rule: the rhythm change delivers the payoff. Use sparingly, once or twice per beat at most.

**6. Voice swap**
Template: "[Narrator sets up the moment.] [Archive clip / reporter / official statement carries the emotion.]"
Rule: let a real voice say the emotional line so the narrator stays flat. If you have no clips, use a short, verified, attributed quote from a source, or omit.

**7. Plain statement**
Template: "[Consequence], and [the painful fact]."
Rule: state the worst fact in one unadorned sentence after the build-up. No adjectives, no commentary. The preceding sentences did the work.

## 3. A worked payoff/open chain (generic)

Illustrative, not from any real script:

| # | Sentence (paraphrased intent) | Pays | Opens |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | In [year], [company] ran [ordinary thing] that ended with [serious outcome]. | What happened? | How? |
| 2 | The idea was simple: [rule]. | What was it? | Why does anyone care? |
| 3 | The prize was worth [translated value]. | Why care? | How far would people go? |
| 4 | Families [behavior]; many [more extreme behavior]. | How far? | What happens at that scale? |
| 5 | By [company's own count], [scale statistic]. | Scale | What does the company do next? |
| 6 | So the company [decision that causes the problem]. | The decision | What goes wrong? |
| 7 | This is where things went wrong. | (bare hook) | What? |
| 8 | [Company] controlled [system]; [only X could happen]. | Why it should have been safe | What breaks it? |
| 9 | Then [failure]. | Something broke | What exactly? |
| 10 | [Mechanism of the failure]. | The cause | How bad? |
| 11 | [Number-last punch]. | How bad | What does the company do? |

## 4. Loops at three lengths

Run all three at once.

- **Micro (one to two sentences):** a fact plus a flip. Example: the happy frame followed by the reversal.
- **Mid (five to ten sentences):** a bare hook, then a delayed explainer, then the break. Example: "things went wrong" paid off by the system failing.
- **Long (half the script or more):** a thread opened early and paid off near the end. Examples: a second story that returns when the two collide, or a question like "will the company ever pay?" opened at the riot and paid off by a surprisingly small fine.

**Overlap rule:** open the next loop before the previous one closes. If a loop closes and nothing new is open, the viewer has a natural exit.

**Honest deflation:** one loop may be deliberately dropped when the evidence runs out (an allegation raised, denied, and left unresolved). Do not manufacture a payoff. Deflating one claim honestly makes the confirmed claims more credible.

## 5. Pacing: slowing down and compressing

- **Slow down** at pivots: give the exact date and time, the meeting at night, the single moment of decision. Scene-level detail signals "this matters."
- **Compress** everything else into one sentence with a time marker: "over the next year", "two days later", "in 2006."
- Keep the triumph tiny (one to three sentences) and the fall large.
- After a long explanatory run, drop in a very short sentence.

## 6. Using other voices

Real voices break monotony, prove the story is real, and carry feeling. Use them at three moments: the triumph (a news announcement), the unexpected turn (a reporter reacting live), and the human cost (a line that makes the viewer imagine the situation). Keep each to one to three lines. When a transcript of a clip is available, paraphrase or quote only short, attributed fragments, and never invent a clip.
